# Glass-substrate and TGV research methodology

Use this reference for glass-substrate, glass-core, TGV, PLP, CoPoS, glass-interposer, or advanced-packaging glass reports. It records a reusable research method, not current article conclusions, prices, company recommendations, or dated commercialization forecasts.

## Research model

Analyze the chain as:

'application need → material advantage → process bottleneck → equipment/material supplier → qualification and yield → usable volume → customer adoption → revenue and margin'

The central question is not merely whether glass is technically attractive. It is whether the technology can pass reliability, yield, throughput, cost, customer qualification, and system-integration requirements at a useful volume.

Always distinguish:

- temporary glass carrier, which leaves the package after processing;
- glass-core substrate, which remains in the package;
- glass interposer, which requires the highest density and integration level.

Do not treat the three applications as one market or assume that success in a carrier proves substrate or interposer commercialization.

## Application and process sequence

### 1. Define the application

Record package size, warpage requirement, signal-speed requirement, line width/spacing, thermal budget, optical-integration need, and whether the glass is temporary or permanent.

### 2. Map the process

Use this process map:

'glass composition and forming → TGV formation → seed layer and metallization → via fill → surface adhesion and wiring → ABF or RDL build-up → dicing → inspection and reliability'

For each step, record the technical objective, defect mode, required material or tool, current development stage, and downstream owner.

### 3. Evaluate the material thesis

Test whether the claimed advantage is measurable:

- CTE matching and warpage;
- flatness, thickness uniformity, and rigidity;
- high-temperature and chemical stability;
- dielectric loss and signal integrity;
- transparency and optical integration;
- crack resistance and mechanical reliability.

Material advantage is not enough if downstream metallization, adhesion, inspection, or dicing removes the benefit.

### 4. Evaluate TGV and metallization

Check:

- hole diameter, depth, taper, aspect ratio, roughness, and crack rate;
- laser modification and wet or dry etch compatibility;
- seed-layer adhesion, coverage, and continuity;
- PVD versus electroless route;
- copper plating, copper-pillar, or resin-fill route;
- voids, delamination, CTE mismatch, and thermal-cycle reliability.

### 5. Evaluate surface build-up, dicing, and inspection

For ABF or RDL build-up, check adhesion, layer count, line width/spacing, symmetry, and warpage. For dicing, check whether the process handles glass, copper, dielectric, and composite-panel stress together. For inspection, check transparent-material optical interference, internal cracks, voids, delamination, electrical continuity, flatness, and dimensional control.

## Bottleneck and supplier-role matrix

| Role | What to verify | Common false positive |
|---|---|---|
| Glass material supplier | composition, forming method, CTE, flatness, thickness, crack resistance, sample size, supply scale | material sample treated as qualified package material |
| TGV processor | hole geometry, crack rate, etch route, throughput, yield, customer qualification | laboratory process treated as production |
| Metallization and via-fill supplier | adhesion, coverage, void rate, thermal reliability, plating or copper-pillar throughput | a process diagram treated as a validated product |
| Substrate or interposer maker | ABF/RDL build-up, line width/spacing, layer count, warpage, customer design-in | existing organic-substrate capability assumed to transfer without glass qualification |
| Panel manufacturer | large-panel handling, exposure, wet process, automation, idle capacity, yield | idle panel capacity treated as advanced-package capacity |
| Foundry or advanced-packaging integrator | package architecture, customer design, pilot line, reliability, production schedule | public demo treated as customer volume adoption |
| Equipment supplier | tool shipment, installation, process qualification, repeat orders, service revenue | purchase order treated as installed productive capacity |
| Materials and chemical supplier | product specification, purity, qualification, recurring consumption, customer share | being named in a supply-chain map treated as actual revenue |
| Inspection and metrology supplier | defect coverage, measurement repeatability, throughput, customer acceptance | prototype measurement system treated as production standard |

Mark every supplier as explicit, inferred, or unverified. The report's supply-chain map is a hypothesis until company disclosure or current operating evidence supports it.

## Commercialization evidence

Use a staged ladder:

1. technology concept or public demonstration;
2. laboratory or pilot process;
3. sample delivery;
4. customer reliability and design validation;
5. small-volume shipment;
6. qualified production line;
7. repeat orders and usable production volume;
8. revenue, utilization, margin, and cash-flow contribution.

For each company, record the highest stage supported by evidence and the next milestone required. Tool development, sample delivery, customer validation, and mass production must remain separate.

## Invalidation triggers

Re-evaluate the thesis when:

- TGV crack, void, delamination, warpage, or inspection defects remain unresolved;
- throughput or yield is materially below the competing substrate route;
- total package cost is too high for the claimed application;
- customers delay design-in or adopt another substrate/interposer route;
- pilot-line announcements do not become installed tools, qualified volume, or repeat orders;
- existing substrate, panel, or foundry capacity cannot be adapted economically;
- product qualification is limited to a demo, a single customer, or a non-recurring engineering project;
- reported opportunity grows but the company's related revenue, utilization, margin, or cash flow does not follow.

## Output requirements

For every glass-substrate report or company, produce:

1. application scope: carrier, substrate, or interposer;
2. material advantage and measurable requirement;
3. process map and bottleneck;
4. supplier role and explicit/inferred status;
5. qualification, yield, throughput, and commercialization stage;
6. catalysts, invalidation triggers, and time horizon;
7. historical report claims separated from current metrics and official disclosures.

Apply the common claim types and validation statuses from the research contract. Never treat a company being named in a supply-chain map as a recommendation.
