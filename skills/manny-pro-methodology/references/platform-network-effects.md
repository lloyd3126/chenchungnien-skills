# Platform and network-effects research methodology

Use this reference for businesses where the value of the service depends on the number, quality, activity, trust, or data contribution of other participants. It applies to payment networks, social platforms, loyalty ecosystems, marketplaces, and auction markets. A loyalty program or large installed base is not automatically a network.

## Research model

Separate the platform into sides and loops:

`side A participation → more liquidity/data/trust → better experience for side B → side B participation → monetization`

Do not call a company a network-effect business merely because it has many users. The network effect must explain why an additional participant improves the product, lowers cost, raises conversion, or increases switching cost for other participants.

Keep three mechanisms separate: a true cross-side network effect, a scale economy, and a loyalty/switching-cost program. For example, hotel members may create direct-booking leverage and points float without improving other members' utility; call it a network effect only when added properties, partners, or members measurably improve cross-side participation.

## Sequence

### 1. Define the sides and the job

Identify each side, the job it hires the platform to do, the alternative it would use without the platform, and whether usage is transactional, recurring, entertainment, informational, or regulatory.

### 2. Measure liquidity and density

Track the probability that a participant finds a suitable counterparty, content, route, product, or audience. Use geography, category, time, quality tier, and user cohort where relevant. Aggregate users can conceal a deteriorating local or category-level network.

### 3. Separate participation from monetization

Build a driver tree such as:

- `active users × engagement × monetizable inventory × price/ARPU`;
- `active buyers × active sellers × match rate × take rate`;
- `payment acceptance × transactions × volume × fee`.

Check whether monetization improves the product or extracts value until participants leave.

### 4. Inspect governance and data loops

For social or community platforms, analyze who sets rules, who enforces them, how algorithms intervene, and whether data is a durable asset or a liability. For payment or loyalty networks, analyze standards, fraud controls, acceptance, and identity.

## Role-specific metrics

| Platform | Primary metrics | Common false positive |
|---|---|---|
| Payment network | acceptance, active accounts, transactions, volume, cross-border use, fraud | scale mistaken for switching cost |
| Social/community | DAU/MAU, session frequency, D/M, content supply, moderation, ARPU | DAU growth without monetizable engagement |
| Marketplace | buyer/seller liquidity, match rate, conversion, repeat, take rate | registered users mistaken for active liquidity |
| Loyalty ecosystem | active members, direct-booking share, redemption, partner/property coverage, points liability, partner purchases | loyalty, float, or brand scale mislabeled as a network effect |
| Auction market | qualified buyers, scarce consignments, sale-through, repeat clients, price discovery | high headline GMV from one-off lots |

## Invalidation triggers

- activity grows but match rate, conversion, repeat rate, or user quality falls;
- participants multi-home more easily and the platform loses pricing power;
- moderation, fraud, or trust failures reduce high-value participation;
- algorithm changes increase engagement but reduce advertiser, seller, or buyer value;
- data licensing or privacy changes remove the monetization loop;
- subsidies are required indefinitely to maintain both sides.

## Output requirements

Show the two-sided or multi-sided map, identify the actual positive feedback loop, calculate at least one density or liquidity metric, separate activity from monetization, and compare the claimed network effect with scale, loyalty, brand, and contractual switching costs. State the smallest observation that would falsify the network-effect claim.
