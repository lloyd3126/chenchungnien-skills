# Acquisition-compounding research methodology

Use this reference for serial acquirers or companies that use acquisitions as a primary reinvestment engine. The goal is to distinguish genuine compounding from leverage, multiple expansion, accounting accretion, or an ever-larger acquisition requirement.

## Research model

Analyze the loop:

`cash generation → target sourcing → purchase price → financing → integration/governance → cash return → next acquisition`

A serial-acquisition strategy compounds only when the buyer can repeatedly find targets, pay rational prices, preserve operating economics, and reinvest at attractive incremental returns.

## Sequence

### 1. Define the acquisition engine

Record target size, geography, vertical, recurring-revenue profile, customer concentration, owner motivation, and the buyer's sourcing advantage. Separate tuck-ins, transformational deals, and capital-market transactions.

### 2. Reconstruct deal economics

For each meaningful acquisition, estimate purchase price, assumed debt, financing cost, acquired revenue/EBITDA/FCF, integration cost, retention, synergies, and time to cash contribution. Calculate a post-tax IRR or cash-on-cash return with conservative assumptions.

Also reconstruct the cash-return measure used by the case rather than relying on EPS accretion. A useful Roper-style starting point is:

`cash earnings ≈ net income + D&A − maintenance capex`

`CRI = cash earnings ÷ gross investment`

Define gross investment consistently, including required working capital, PP&E and relevant accumulated depreciation, purchase price, and acquisition accounting when testing the buyer's total return. Keep organic operating returns separate from acquisition-inclusive returns.

### 3. Test the supply of targets

Ask whether the addressable target pool is large enough, whether private-equity competition is rising, whether the buyer's valuation can support stock-funded deals, and whether management's hurdle rate has changed.

### 4. Check decentralized governance

Assess capital-allocation authority, incentive design, autonomy, reporting discipline, integration requirements, and the point at which scale adds bureaucracy or reduces entrepreneurial performance.

## Role-specific metrics

| Check | Primary metrics | Common false positive |
|---|---|---|
| Cash engine | FCF, maintenance capex, cash return, debt capacity | all FCF treated as available for acquisitions |
| Deal quality | purchase multiple, IRR, retention, organic growth, margin | EPS accretion mistaken for value creation |
| Pipeline | target count, average size, sector supply, competition, prices | historical deal pace extrapolated indefinitely |
| Governance | autonomy, manager retention, incentive alignment, overhead | decentralization claimed without decision evidence |
| Scale | recurring revenue, purchasing power, shared costs, integration burden | scale benefits assumed without margin or retention evidence |

## Invalidation triggers

- purchase multiples rise while target growth, retention, or returns fall;
- organic growth slows and reported growth depends increasingly on acquisitions;
- debt, goodwill, or dilution rises faster than FCF;
- management lowers return hurdles or changes definitions of cash earnings;
- target supply shrinks or competition makes the historical strategy uneconomic;
- acquired managers leave and customer retention or margins deteriorate.

## Output requirements

Produce a deal-pipeline map, normalized FCF/maintenance-capex bridge, CRI or equivalent cash-return calculation, sample deal IRRs, financing and dilution analysis, organic versus acquired growth, governance assessment, scale risks, and a base case that assumes a slower acquisition pace. Do not call a company a compounder without proving acquisition-inclusive reinvestment returns.
