# Competitive entry-point and substitution methodology

Use this reference when a business appears protected at the company level but competitors can enter through one customer group, workflow step, distribution channel, product tier, geography, or alternative technical rail.

## Research model

Map competition as:

`entry point → controlled customer or workflow → changed transaction path → shifted value capture → incumbent response → new equilibrium`

The important question is not merely who the competitors are. It is which relationship or bottleneck they control, which part of the incumbent's economics they can bypass, and whether they complement the incumbent before becoming a substitute.

## Sequence

### 1. Draw the incumbent path

Mark the customer's job, decision maker, user, payer, distributor, data owner, capital provider, regulatory gate, and each step where switching can occur. Identify the incumbent's value-capture point and the minimum capabilities required to bypass it.

### 2. Classify the entry point

Classify each entrant by consumer side, merchant/provider side, both sides, workflow layer, low-price tier, adjacent product, distribution, regulation, or alternative technology. Record whether the entrant initially adds volume to the incumbent, negotiates away margin, or replaces the incumbent's rail.

### 3. Test substitution rather than similarity

Compare total customer outcome: price, speed, credit, convenience, trust, integration, compliance, service quality, and failure risk. A technically similar product is not a substitute until customers can adopt it in the relevant workflow and counterparties accept it.

### 4. Trace value redistribution

Build before-and-after transaction maps. Estimate which participant gains revenue, data, customer ownership, or negotiating leverage and which participant absorbs new cost, capital, or risk. Distinguish demand growth from incumbent share loss.

### 5. Model the incumbent response

Test price cuts, bundling, open interfaces, exclusivity, acquisition, litigation, product segmentation, or migration to a new rail. Include cannibalization and the possibility that defending the bottleneck lowers take rate or ROIC.

## Role-specific checks

| Entry pattern | Primary checks | Common false positive |
|---|---|---|
| Payment or transaction rail | acceptance, routing, settlement, credit, compliance, take rate | technical volume mistaken for retail substitution |
| Workflow/hardware wedge | installed base, attach, utilization, interoperability, downstream revenue | device adoption mistaken for workflow control |
| Low-price product tier | customer overlap, churn, mix, gross margin, service outcome | category growth hiding incumbent share loss |
| Distribution/aggregator | customer ownership, traffic source, commission, data access | partner volume assumed permanently complementary |
| Regulatory entry | license state, permitted product, tax/fee, launch readiness | legal eligibility mistaken for operating market access |

## Invalidation triggers

- an entrant gains users but cannot gain acceptance, workflow integration, trust, or economic distribution;
- the incumbent remains a required rail even when the front-end brand changes;
- lower price does not compensate customers for inferior outcome, compliance, or switching cost;
- the entrant grows the category without reducing incumbent share or take rate;
- the incumbent's response preserves volume only by permanently worsening margin or capital intensity;
- a supposed replacement depends on the incumbent's infrastructure, data, or license.

## Output requirements

Produce an incumbent path, entry-point matrix, before/after value-capture map, substitution scorecard, incumbent-response scenarios, and leading indicators that distinguish complement, bargaining threat, and full replacement.
